# Research 06 — Competitive Landscape & Patent Survey

*Phase 1 research document. Scope: the commercial and open-source state of the art in adaptive endurance-run coaching, and the patent/IP terrain the system must navigate. This document is a landscape and freedom-to-operate survey; it complements the mechanism-level research in [`research/01-exercise-physiology.md`], [`research/02-wearable-data-garmin.md`], [`research/04-coaching-periodization.md`], and [`research/05-data-to-adaptation.md`], and does not restate their formulas. Where a competitor's method is genuinely a candidate the system could adopt or extend, this doc points to it and defers the derivation to doc 05.*

*Reading guide: §1–§8 are the descriptive landscape and freedom-to-operate survey. §9 and §10 are the action-oriented core — **what to extend and what to borrow** — and are the sections a Phase 2 implementer should treat as requirements input. §11 is an FTO addendum on the conversational-coach interface, added when that decision was taken (`decisions/01`).*

## 1. Why this survey exists

The project's design thesis — derive our own load, fatigue, and readiness metrics from raw signals rather than lean on vendor black boxes (see project instructions, "Metrics philosophy — raw over derived") — is a deliberate bet against the mainstream of the industry. Almost every shipping product does the opposite: it computes a small number of proprietary summary scores and drives coaching decisions off them. To make that bet responsibly we need to know three things: (1) what the competitors actually do, so we are not reinventing worse versions of solved problems; (2) which of their methods rest on *open, published* science we can legitimately build on, versus proprietary algorithms we should treat only as corroboration; and (3) where the patents lie, so the implementation does not unknowingly replicate a claimed method. This document addresses all three and ends with the differentiation argument for our raw-data-first approach.

A note on confidence: product methodology descriptions below are drawn from vendor documentation, support articles, and reputable third-party analysis. Vendors deliberately under-document their algorithms, so several descriptions are necessarily at the "what it uses / what it claims" level rather than "here is the formula." That opacity is itself the finding — it is the reason the project prefers open methods — and it is flagged wherever it applies. Patent summaries are drawn from the published patent record (Google Patents / Justia) and should be confirmed with counsel before any freedom-to-operate decision; this is engineering research, not a legal opinion.

## 2. A four-layer taxonomy of the field

The competitive field is easier to reason about split into four layers, because the layers make different promises and expose different amounts of their method.

The **device-and-OS ecosystems** — Garmin (with Firstbeat), COROS, Polar, Suunto, Apple, WHOOP — own the sensor and therefore the raw data. They ship on-device derived metrics (VO2max estimate, training status, recovery time, readiness, strain) and, increasingly, on-device adaptive workout suggestions. Their algorithms are the most closed and the most patent-protected, and they are exactly the vendor-derived numbers the project instructions tell us to demote to a secondary role.

The **analytics platforms** — TrainingPeaks / WKO5, Golden Cheetah, Runalyze, intervals.icu — ingest data from the ecosystems and expose transparent, well-documented load models (TSS, the Performance Management Chart, the Banister impulse-response model, critical power modelling). They largely do *not* generate or adapt plans automatically; they instrument a human coach or a self-coached athlete. This layer is where most of the *open, citable* methodology lives, and it is the layer our derived-metric work most resembles.

The **AI adaptive-coaching apps** — Runna, Athletica, Humango, TriDot / RunDot, AI Endurance, Vinco, 2ndWind, Trenara — are the direct product competitors: they generate a full plan for a goal race and adapt it as data arrives. This is precisely the product the project is specifying. Their methods range from lightly-adaptive templates (Runna) to genuinely model-driven engines grounded in published sports science (Athletica) to proprietary data-model prediction (TriDot).

The **open / DIY tooling** — Golden Cheetah, Runalyze, and the R/Python modelling community around the Banister and critical-power models — is not a commercial competitor but is the reference implementation of the transparent methods, and is directly reusable as a specification and validation target.

## 3. Layer 1 — Device and OS ecosystems

### 3.1 Garmin + Firstbeat (the incumbent to beat)

Garmin is the project's primary data source (see doc 02) and also its most complete functional competitor, because since acquiring **Firstbeat Technologies in June 2020** it ships a full derived-metric stack plus **Daily Suggested Workouts (DSW)**, an on-watch adaptive coach. Understanding this stack matters twice over: it is the competition, and it is the source of the vendor-derived numbers we are choosing not to trust as a foundation.

The Firstbeat stack rests on a chain of estimations. **Fitness Level / VO2max** is estimated from heart rate and speed during free-form running, segmenting the activity into coherent-intensity sections, scoring each segment's reliability, and regressing heart rate against modelled VO2 (this is the method claimed in the Firstbeat "Fitness test" patent, §6). **Training load** is quantified through **EPOC** (Excess Post-exercise Oxygen Consumption) modelled from heart rate, which Firstbeat converts into its **Training Effect** (aerobic and anaerobic) scores — again a patented method (§6). **Training Status, Recovery Time, HRV Status, and Training Readiness** are further derived layers built on top, with Training Readiness synthesising sleep, HRV status, recovery time, and acute load into a single readiness signal.

Daily Suggested Workouts then combines these: VO2max anchors the intensity/pace zones, Training Status drives session-type selection (a "productive" status yields harder suggestions), acute-versus-chronic load and Recovery Time gate whether a hard session is offered at all, and a race goal entered in Garmin Connect biases session types toward that event's demands. The published limitations are instructive for our design: DSW is "only as accurate as the data it receives," optical-HR error is the largest error source, and *unrecorded* sessions are invisible to the load model and produce misaligned suggestions.

Two things stand out for us. First, Garmin's architecture is exactly the dual-load framework doc 04/05 describe — internal load (HR-derived) plus external load (pace/distance) — so our own load model will resemble the *shape* of theirs while replacing the proprietary EPOC/Training-Effect core with open, documented formulas. Second, every one of these outputs is a black box that "changes without notice and is not independently validated" (project instructions), and independent validation of the VO2max estimate in particular is mixed. This is the concrete justification for the raw-over-derived philosophy: we can consume Garmin's *raw* FIT streams (HR, pace, RR intervals, running dynamics) with confidence and treat Garmin's *derived* numbers (VO2max, Training Status, Body Battery, Readiness) as, at most, corroborating signals.

Garmin also ships **Race Predictor**, **PacePro** (pacing strategy over a course elevation profile), **Endurance Score**, **Hill Score**, and **Stamina** — all Firstbeat-lineage, all relevant because the project must translate a goal race into a pace/effort target and model course elevation and environment. Race Predictor is widely observed to over-predict marathon capability for athletes without a deep long-run history, which is a useful cautionary data point for our own race-time model (doc 05).

### 3.2 COROS (EvoLab)

COROS's **EvoLab** computes a **Running Fitness** score (40–100) over a rolling 6-week window and decomposes ability into four bands — Base (aerobic endurance, evaluated near the lowest aerobic-power pace, relevant to >30 km), Endurance (lactate-threshold pace, ~10 km), Speed (VO2max, ~3 km), and Sprint (anaerobic, 30–60 s max pace). This is effectively a pace/duration profile — conceptually adjacent to a critical-speed curve — surfaced as consumer metrics, and it feeds training-pace zones and a race predictor. COROS documents the *inputs and structure* but not the estimation math, so it is a design reference, not a citable formula source. Notably, COROS positions much of EvoLab as free and derived from device data, which sets a market expectation that fitness estimation is table-stakes, not a premium feature.

### 3.3 WHOOP (strain / recovery, readiness-first)

WHOOP is the purest expression of the readiness-first philosophy and the project's clearest *opposite number* on metrics strategy. It computes a daily **Recovery** score (driven heavily by HRV, resting HR, sleep) and a **Strain** score (a 0–21 logarithmic scale of cardiovascular load derived from heart rate), then issues a **Strain Target** / AI-coached recommendation for how hard to go today given recovery. It has no GPS/pace pedigree and does not build periodized race plans, so it is not a head-to-head plan competitor — but its recovery-gated daily-recommendation loop is exactly the adaptation pattern our system must implement, and its patent portfolio (§6) is the most relevant to the readiness half of our design. WHOOP's weakness from our vantage point is that Strain is intensity-agnostic to *type* (it does not distinguish a threshold session from a long run of equal cardiovascular cost) — a gap our workout-representation and load model should close.

## 4. Layer 2 — Analytics platforms (where the open science lives)

### 4.1 TrainingPeaks / WKO5 — the Performance Management Chart

The **Performance Management Chart (PMC)** is the single most influential open framework in the field and the reference our own load/fatigue/form metrics should be specified against and differentiated from (full derivation belongs in doc 05; summarised here for competitive context). It is Andrew Coggan's operationalisation of **Banister's 1975 impulse-response model** and defines three metrics from a per-session **Training Stress Score (TSS)**:

- **TSS** = duration(hours) × IF² × 100, where **IF** (Intensity Factor) = normalized power ÷ functional threshold power. For running, the pace-based analogue is **rTSS** (using threshold pace / Grade Adjusted Pace) and the HR-based analogue is **hrTSS** / TRIMP.
- **CTL** (Chronic Training Load, "fitness") = exponentially-weighted moving average of daily TSS with a **42-day** time constant.
- **ATL** (Acute Training Load, "fatigue") = EWMA of daily TSS with a **7-day** time constant.
- **TSB** (Training Stress Balance, "form/freshness") = CTL − ATL (typically yesterday's values).

Coggan deliberately dropped Banister's gain terms (kₐ, k_f), trading absolute performance prediction for a robust, interpretable "fitness/fatigue/form" dashboard — "part science, part art." This is the crucial competitive insight for us: the PMC is *transparent, published, and un-patented as a concept*, which is exactly why it has become the industry lingua franca. Our system can adopt the CTL/ATL/TSB structure directly and legitimately, and then *improve* on it — the well-documented weaknesses (a single scalar load conflates very different stimuli; fixed 42/7-day constants are not individualized; garbage-in from bad threshold estimates) are the openings doc 05 already targets. The Banister model's own documented limitations (parameter instability, over-fitting, poor out-of-sample prediction — see the PMC1974899 critique) are why we treat CTL/ATL as monitoring signals, not as a performance oracle.

### 4.2 Golden Cheetah, Runalyze, intervals.icu

These open/freemium platforms are the reference implementations. **Golden Cheetah** ships an open implementation of the Banister impulse-response model and critical-power modelling; **Runalyze** offers open VO2max-style effective-VO2 tracking, TRIMP, and marathon-shape metrics; **intervals.icu** reproduces the PMC (Fitness/Fatigue/Form) for free off Garmin/Strava data. For the project these are gold: they demonstrate that every metric we need can be computed transparently from raw data, they give us validation baselines to test our implementation against, and they confirm freedom to operate on the core load-modelling math (the methods are decades-old and openly published).

### 4.3 Stryd — running power and the critical-power model

Stryd is the leading running-**power** product and the most rigorous consumer implementation of the **Critical Power (CP)** model for runners. Its **Power Duration Curve** characterises sustainable power across durations; **Critical Power** (the asymptote, effectively a ~30–70 min sustainable power) plus the finite work capacity above it (W′) define the athlete's profile. Race predictions convert the PDC and CP into a race-power target and finishing-time estimate, adjusted by a **fatigue factor** (from age, sex, and PDC slope — steep slope = more anaerobic/sprint-oriented), and by **course elevation, temperature, and humidity** — directly relevant to the project's requirement to model course profile and environment. Stryd uses the last ~90 days of data and predicts "as if tapered."

The CP/W′ model is open, peer-reviewed science (the underlying critical-power literature, and validation studies such as PMC10286607 on whether Stryd CP is a meaningful parameter — the evidence is supportive but with caveats). So critical-speed/critical-power modelling is a legitimate, citable candidate for our state model (doc 05 should weigh CP-from-pace against CP-from-power, since most of our target athletes will *not* own a Stryd pod and we must derive an equivalent from raw pace/GPS + grade). Stryd's *specific* device and algorithm implementations may be protected, but the CP model itself is not ours to avoid — it is public domain science.

## 5. Layer 3 — AI adaptive-coaching apps (the direct competitors)

These are the products a user would choose *instead of* what we are building. The important axis is how genuinely adaptive each is, and on what basis it adapts.

### 5.1 Runna — market leader, lightly adaptive

Runna is the current consumer market leader for run-plan generation. Tellingly, it "rather than using proprietary fitness scores" sets initial paces directly from the athlete's reported ability level and recent race times, then makes *conservative, rule-based* progressions: mileage rises on a week-by-week curve whose maximum increase is a function of current mileage (protecting high-mileage runners, allowing faster ramp for low-mileage), with a "double constraint" for beginners. Adaptation is real but modest — it monitors completed workouts, suggests pace up/down adjustments, accounts for interruptions, and (as of mid-2026) auto-adjusts target paces for **heat and humidity**. It also added illness-response plan adaptation. Runna's lesson for us is double-edged: its enormous adoption proves that *rule-based, transparent progression with light data-driven pace nudges* is commercially sufficient and trusted — but its shallow physiological modelling is precisely the space a raw-data-first, state-estimating engine can outperform on the project's success metric (average race-day pace).

### 5.2 Athletica — the closest philosophical competitor

Athletica is the most scientifically-grounded direct competitor and the one whose design most resembles the project's ambition. It is built on **Laursen & Buchheit's *Science and Application of High-Intensity Interval Training* (2018)** by those authors themselves, and it explicitly ingests **power, pace, HR, resting HR, HRV, sleep, session structure, execution, and RPE**, tracks fitness/fatigue/consistency trends, and adapts future load off completed sessions, missed sessions, and physiological response. It markets itself as principle-driven rather than "black-box heuristics." Athletica validates the project's core thesis — that a defensible, published-science, multi-signal adaptive engine is buildable and marketable — while leaving room to differentiate on (a) Garmin-first raw-stream depth, (b) fully *specified/owned* derived metrics rather than a still-proprietary engine, and (c) the single-minded average-race-pace optimisation target.

### 5.3 TriDot / RunDot — proprietary data-model prediction

TriDot (triathlon) and its running spin-off **RunDot** are the most aggressively "AI/data" of the competitors and the most closed. The core is **Normalized Training Stress (NTS)** — a proprietary alternative to TSS that claims to fold in discipline, **environmental conditions**, intensity distribution, and an individual **Training Stress Profile**, accumulating into **Normalized Training Load**. TriDot's pitch is "Optimized Training" and race prediction (RaceX) from a large proprietary athlete dataset. Everything is proprietary and unvalidated externally, which makes it a *marketing* benchmark (environmental normalization and individualized stress response are clearly features users value and we should match) but not a *methodological* source we can cite or copy. Its environmental-normalization emphasis reinforces that our environment model (temp/humidity/wind, per project inputs) is competitively important, not optional.

### 5.4 Humango, AI Endurance, and the rest

**Humango** offers AI multisport plans that adapt to life/schedule and completed sessions and can co-exist with a human coach; **AI Endurance** publicly documents an **HRV-based recovery model** and ML race prediction; **Trenara**, **Vinco**, and **2ndWind** round out the run/tri AI-coach category. Collectively they establish the feature baseline our spec must meet or beat: goal-race plan generation, calendar/life adaptation, completed-session response, readiness/HRV gating, and race-time prediction. None publishes enough method to serve as a citation source; their value is competitive positioning.

## 7. What the whole field shares (and what that implies for our architecture)

Across all four layers, the successful products converge on the same three-stage loop, which validates the loop the project instructions require (raw data → derived metrics → state estimate → plan change):

**State estimation.** Everyone estimates a current fitness/threshold anchor (VO2max, critical speed/power, threshold pace, or a fitness score) plus a fatigue/readiness state (CTL/ATL/TSB, recovery score, HRV status). The differentiator is transparency: the ecosystems hide it, the analytics platforms expose it, and the AI apps sit in between. **Our position: expose and own it** — every anchor and every readiness signal specified from raw data.

**Plan generation.** Everyone maps the goal race + current state to a periodized structure with pace/effort zones. Rule-based progression (Runna) is commercially sufficient; principle-based, physiology-targeted workout selection (Athletica, and what doc 04 specifies) is the higher bar we should meet.

**Adaptation.** Everyone adjusts on completed-vs-planned execution, acute/chronic load balance, readiness/HRV, and increasingly environment (heat/humidity — Runna, TriDot, Stryd). Illness/interruption handling and calendar/life adaptation are now table-stakes (Runna, Humango). **Our environment model (temp/humidity/wind + course elevation) is competitively required, not a nice-to-have.**

## 8. Differentiation — the argument for our raw-data-first system

The competitive survey sharpens rather than undermines the project thesis. Three defensible wedges emerge:

**Transparency and ownership of every metric.** The market splits into black-box ecosystems (Garmin, WHOOP, COROS) and transparent-but-manual analytics (TrainingPeaks, Golden Cheetah). The AI apps that combine automation with method (Athletica, TriDot) still keep their engines proprietary. A system whose load, fatigue, readiness, and state metrics are *each fully specified and derived from raw FIT streams* occupies a genuinely under-served position: automated like the AI apps, transparent like the analytics platforms, and vendor-independent by construction. This is also the safest FTO posture (§6).

**Raw-signal depth on a Garmin-first pipeline.** Most AI apps abstract away from the device and consume summary data (often via Strava). Specifying against raw FIT fields — RR intervals for our own HRV metric, running dynamics, per-second HR/pace/grade for our own load and durability metrics — lets the system track physiological change the summary-fed competitors cannot see, and sidesteps the specific patented estimation pipelines by using raw inputs into open methods.

**Single-metric optimisation.** No competitor optimises explicitly for one declared success metric. Anchoring every adaptation decision to *expected average race-day pace over the goal distance* (project instructions) gives a cleaner objective function for the adaptation logic than the diffuse "get fitter / stay fresh" framing the field uses — and makes the durability/fatigue-resistance research (doc 01) directly actionable, since late-race pace decay is where average pace is won or lost and where the summary-metric competitors are weakest.

The risks the survey surfaces: Runna proves users will accept — and trust — *simple* progression, so our added modelling complexity must demonstrably pay off in race pace or it is over-engineering; Garmin's own DSW is free and on-wrist, so our value must clearly exceed "what the watch already does"; and every readiness/recovery product struggles with sensor-data quality (optical-HR error, missing sessions), so our data-quality and gap-handling logic (doc 02, doc 05) is a first-class requirement, not an edge case.

## 9. Patents and methods we can extend

The project asked the offensive question directly: *what can we extend?* First a clarification on the verb, because it governs what is legitimate. You cannot "extend" — in the sense of build on and modify — a competitor's **live** patent without a licence; a granted claim in force is a right to exclude. What you *can* do falls into three legitimate moves: **(A) build freely on expired or never-patented public-domain methods**, which is the true "extend" opportunity; **(B) learn from the *teaching* of a live patent and implement the underlying idea by a different, non-infringing construction** — patents are published precisely so the field can learn from them, and the idea a patent teaches is not itself protected, only the specific claimed method; and **(C) file our own improvement patents on the public-domain base**, should IP protection ever become a project goal. Each move is mapped below to concrete targets.

### 9.1 Move A — expired / public-domain foundations we can build on and extend outright

These are the highest-value targets, because they are free to use *and* free to modify, and every one has a documented, citable formulation.

The **Banister impulse-response model and TRIMP** (Banister 1975/1991) were never enforceably patented and are now foundational public-domain science. We adopt the fitness/fatigue two-component structure and **extend** it exactly where the literature says it is weak: replace the fixed 42-/7-day EWMA constants with **per-athlete-fitted time constants**, and split the single scalar impulse into **multiple load channels** (aerobic-metabolic vs. mechanical/durability load) so a long run and a threshold session of equal cardiovascular cost are no longer scored identically — the specific gap we flagged in WHOOP Strain (§3.3) and single-scalar TSS (§4.1). Doc 05 owns the derivation; this is the mandate.

The **Critical Power / Critical Speed model** (Monod & Scherrer 1965; extended by Hughson, Jones, Vanhatalo and others) is likewise public-domain science and the backbone of Stryd's proprietary *implementation* — but the model itself is ours to use. The **extension opportunity** is to derive an equivalent critical-speed and W′ from **raw pace + grade-adjusted pace**, so the athlete gets a Stryd-quality power-duration profile *without owning a Stryd pod* — directly serving our Garmin-first, most-athletes-have-no-power-meter reality — and then to add a **durability term** that lets critical speed decay as a function of accumulated load within a run (doc 01's fatigue-resistance determinant). This is a genuine improvement over the static CP profile the products expose.

The **Polar OwnIndex / non-exercise Fitness Test** is the sleeper target. Polar's method estimates VO2max at rest from **resting HR, heart-rate variability, age, sex, height, weight, and a self-assessed activity level**, via a neural network developed on lab data in **1996–1998**. Because the method dates to the late 1990s, patents of that vintage (20-year term) have lapsed, and the method is additionally documented in Polar's own white paper and multiple validation studies — so it is usable and extensible. Its value to us is specific: it solves the **cold-start problem**. Before we have enough of an athlete's running data to fit a critical-speed curve, a resting-HRV-plus-demographics estimate gives a defensible initial fitness state on day one (the system's own seed takes only the inputs `research/00` COLD-08 names, which exclude HRV), which we then **blend into, and hand off to, our raw-data critical-speed estimate** as running history accumulates. No current competitor combines a non-exercise cold-start estimate with a raw-data running-derived estimate in a principled hand-off — that is an extension worth making.

The **TSS / Performance Management Chart triad (CTL/ATL/TSB)** is, as a *concept*, openly published and un-patented (§4.1), which is why the whole industry uses it. We adopt the interpretable fitness/fatigue/form dashboard wholesale (users trust it, and it is the lingua franca a Phase 2 UI should speak) and **extend** it with the individualized constants and multi-channel load above, plus honest confidence bands rather than false precision.

### 9.2 Move B — live patents whose *idea* we extend via a different construction

The **Firstbeat "Fitness test" patent** (US9237868B2, in force to ~2030) teaches something genuinely smart that we should learn from without copying: real-world free-run data is noisy, so before regressing heart rate against VO2 it **segments the run into coherent-intensity blocks and weights each block by a reliability score**, discarding or down-weighting untrustworthy segments (steep downhills, HR artefacts). The *idea* — quality-weight your raw data before you estimate — is not protectable; the *specific reliability-weighted-segmentation-then-regression pipeline* is. So we adopt the principle in our **critical-speed / threshold estimator** using a **different construction**: score each data window by our own quality signals (GPS/grade validity, HR-drift and cadence-lock flags, steady-state detection from RR intervals) and feed quality-weighted windows into a critical-speed fit rather than Firstbeat's HR↔VO2 regression. Same wisdom, different mechanism, clear of the claim.

The **Firstbeat EPOC / Training Effect** work teaches the value of a **physiologically-scaled** load that **separates aerobic from anaerobic** training effect — better than a flat duration×intensity² TSS. We extend that idea using **open** inputs: an intensity-weighted TRIMP/rTSS for the aerobic channel plus a separate anaerobic contribution derived from time spent above critical speed and W′ depletion — not EPOC.

**WHOOP's** recovery/strain patents (US11602279, US11574722) teach two adoptable ideas: a **daily readiness-gated "how hard today" recommendation**, and a **logarithmic strain scale** that better reflects the disproportionate cost of high intensity. We implement readiness-gating from open HRV literature (Ln rMSSD rolling means and coefficient-of-variation — the Plews/Altini/Kubios body of work) and can borrow the log-scaling *concept* for how our load metric compresses at the top end, without reproducing WHOOP's specific indicator construction.

**TriDot's NTS** teaches that load should be **environment- and individual-normalized**. We extend that idea with **published, transparent** models — established heat/humidity pace-decrement relationships and grade-adjusted pace — rather than their opaque proprietary normalization, giving us the same benefit auditable.

### 9.3 Move C — where our own novelty could sit

If the project ever wants defensible IP of its own, the white space is visible from the survey: nobody publicly combines (a) a **non-exercise cold-start fitness estimate handed off to a raw-data critical-speed estimate**, (b) a **multi-channel, durability-aware load model with per-athlete time constants**, and (c) an adaptation engine whose **single objective function is expected average race-day pace over a specific course and environment**. Those are improvements on a public-domain base — the cleanest footing for a new claim, and the same three things that make the product better regardless of whether we patent them.

| Feature to adopt | Who does it well | Why it matters (esp. for average race pace) | How we adapt it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Environmental pace/effort adjustment** (heat, humidity, wind) | Runna, Stryd, TriDot | Race-day conditions can cost minutes; hot-weather training also inflates HR and mis-scores load if uncorrected | Open thermal pace-decrement + wind models feeding both the race target and our load metric (doc 05) |
| **Course-elevation pacing plan** (grade-adjusted pace, even-effort split) | Stryd, Garmin PacePro | Average pace over the *specific* course is the metric; even-effort pacing over hills beats even-pace | Compute GAP over the supplied elevation profile; output an even-effort pacing plan as the race target |
| **Readiness/recovery gating of hard sessions** | Garmin DSW, WHOOP | Prevents adaptation-killing overreach and injury; a session done too fatigued yields little and costs recovery | Our HRV+load readiness metric gates whether today's hard session proceeds, scales, or defers |
| **Missed/modified-session re-planning** | Athletica, Humango | Real amateurs miss sessions; a plan that just falls behind loses the athlete | Adaptation logic re-plans forward from actual completed load, not from the original calendar |
| **Conservative, mileage-dependent progression caps** ("double constraint") | Runna | An injured athlete's race pace is undefined; guardrails keep the athlete training | Load-ramp guardrails scaled to current volume and frequency; hard caps on weekly increase |
| **Ability decomposition into physiological bands** (base/threshold/VO2/anaerobic) | COROS EvoLab, Stryd PDC | A multi-dimensional state lets us target the *limiting* determinant instead of "get fitter" generally | Our state model exposes the athlete's profile across the power/speed-duration curve, not one number |
| **Race-time prediction with explicit caveats** | Stryd, Garmin (as a cautionary tale) | Needed to translate goal↔target and to detect over-reach; Garmin's marathon over-prediction shows the failure mode | Predict from the durability-aware critical-speed model; weight long-run evidence, publish confidence bands |
| **Rolling-window current fitness** (~6 weeks) | COROS | Estimates must track *current* state, not career-best | Windowed/EWMA estimates with windows justified in doc 05 |
| **Illness / interruption handling** | Runna | A specific, common disruptor of the plan | Explicit return-to-training ramp triggered by a flagged interruption |
| **Fitness / fatigue / form dashboard** | TrainingPeaks PMC | The interpretable triad athletes already understand and trust | Adopt the triad as our primary athlete-facing view, computed transparently |
| **Subjective feedback fusion** (RPE, feel) | Athletica, WHOOP AI Coach | A cheap, high-value signal that corroborates or corrects objective load (ties to doc 03) | Fuse session-RPE and wellness check-ins into readiness and into load-validation |
| **A concrete daily "dose" recommendation** (strain/effort target) | WHOOP | Turns state into an actionable number the athlete can execute today | Map readiness → today's target session intensity/duration, expressed as pace+effort |

Two "learn what *not* to do" lessons round out the list. First, **do not over-trust a single scalar** (WHOOP Strain, plain TSS) — it conflates stimuli our multi-channel load model must keep distinct. Second, **do not ship opacity**: every product that hides its method pays for it in user distrust and in our inability to validate it; our transparency is a feature, not just a compliance posture. And the humbling counter-lesson from Runna's market lead: **simplicity that athletes trust beats sophistication they don't** — so each borrowed complexity must visibly pay off in the race-pace objective or be cut.

## 11. Conversational-interface freedom-to-operate (addendum)

*Added when the Conversational Coach Interface decision was taken (`decisions/01-conversational-coach-interface.md`), to answer that note's open follow-up: is conversational coaching heavily patented in a way that constrains the design or undermines a "differentiator" claim? This addendum covers only the conversational chat layer — the natural-language surface that explains engine decisions and translates athlete free-text into the structured inputs the deterministic engine consumes. The FTO of the underlying load/fitness/readiness models is §6; nothing below re-examines the engine itself.*

**Overall finding.** Conversational and "virtual coach" interfaces are a **very crowded, decades-old patent space**, but the crowding cuts in our favour rather than against us. The density is dominated by (a) broad, foundational conversational-AI / virtual-assistant patents (Siri-class intelligent automated assistants, generic chatbot and dialogue-management patents) that are not fitness-specific, and (b) a smaller cluster of "virtual health coach" and "virtual coaching platform" patents that pair a conversational agent with *coaching decision-making*. The key observation for our design is that almost every coaching-specific patent in this space claims a system where the conversational agent itself **generates, personalizes, or decides** the coaching plan or guidance. Our architecture deliberately does the opposite: the LLM is confined to translation and explanation, and a separate deterministic engine makes every training decision. That separation is the principal thing that keeps us clear of the coaching-specific claims. There is also extensive **non-patent prior art** — published academic systems for LLM-based conversational exercise coaching (PlanFitting, GPTCoach, CoachAI) — which both confirms the concept is not novel to anyone and provides a defensive prior-art backdrop. We did **not** find a patent whose claims squarely capture our specific "LLM explains + translates only, deterministic engine decides, chat is not a confirmation gate" arrangement.

**Patents that matter.** The most on-point coaching-specific grant is **Philips' US10504379B2** (adaptive embodied conversational agent for virtual coaching): it claims an agent that ingests physiological/behavioural/psychological/medical data and *generates a customized coaching plan* delivered through a conversational character. It is distinguishable from us because the claimed novelty is the agent generating the coaching plan and adapting its persona — in our design the plan comes from the deterministic engine and the LLM neither generates nor personalizes coaching content beyond restating engine output. **Verint/Next IT's US9536049B2** (and its family, incl. US9824188B2) is a broad conversational health-assistant patent centred on dialog-based management of medical tasks; it is a healthcare-assistant claim, not a training-plan adaptation claim, and our sports-training scope plus non-medical framing sit outside its core. **BetterUp's US11024189B2** (virtual coaching platform) is executive/well-being coaching built on structured dimensional assessments and coach-matching, not automated fitness plan adaptation — low overlap. The generic-chatbot and virtual-assistant patents (e.g. Apple's intelligent-automated-assistant family, generic "conversational agent" and chatbot-training patents such as Accenture's US11270081B2, and pending RAG-agent applications such as US20240289863A1) are broad but domain-agnostic; they are a background risk that every product with a chatbot carries, not a fitness-specific threat we can design around by narrowing our chat feature.

*Broad foundational conversational-AI patents* (e.g. Apple's intelligent-automated-assistant family such as US9548050B2, and generic "conversational agent" patents like US9971766B2) are noted only as background: they are domain-agnostic virtual-assistant claims that any chatbot-bearing product coexists with, and are not a fitness-specific constraint.

**FTO posture.** The concrete guidance is to keep the architecture exactly as specified and let that architecture — not the chat concept — carry the design. Specifically: (1) keep every training decision inside the deterministic engine and keep the LLM strictly confined to (a) reading the engine's decision log and computed metrics and restating them in plain language, and (b) parsing free-text into the structured schema the engine already consumes — never generating novel numbers, plans, or recommendations; (2) ensure the spec and any marketing avoid describing the LLM as "generating," "personalizing," or "deciding" coaching, since those verbs are what the coaching-specific claims (esp. Philips US10504379B2) turn on; (3) treat chat as a non-gating I/O surface (the engine acts regardless of chat), which further separates us from claims that require the conversational agent to be in the decision/confirmation loop — this aligns with the authority document's §1.8 (chat is not a gate); (4) document the published academic prior art (PlanFitting, GPTCoach, CoachAI) as evidence that conversational exercise coaching is well-known and unpatentable in the abstract; and (5) do not attempt to claim, or rely on, novelty in "a conversational fitness coach" — that ground is thoroughly occupied and would be both hard to defend and unnecessary.

**Differentiator reframing.** Given how crowded conversational coaching is, our differentiator is **not** "a chat coach" — many exist (WHOOP Coach, Fitbit/Gemini, Peloton, iFIT, and a long tail of apps) — but the **transparent, raw-data-derived, deterministic five-timescale adaptation engine underneath**, which the chat layer merely exposes and explains; the novelty and defensibility live in the engine, not the conversation. This is consistent with the §8 differentiation argument and with the authority document's keystones.

**Caveat.** This is engineering research compiled from the public patent record (Google Patents, Justia) and is **not legal advice**. Patent statuses, claim scope, continuations, and assignments change, and "reads on / distinguishable" judgments here are preliminary. Freedom to operate must be confirmed by qualified patent counsel with a full claim-chart analysis before relying on any conclusion above.
