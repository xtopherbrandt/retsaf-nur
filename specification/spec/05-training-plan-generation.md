# Section 5 — Training-Plan Generation

*Build-ready specification, Phase 2. This section defines the layer that turns an estimate of the athlete (Section 4) and a goal race (Section 1) into a concrete, prescriptive, machine-representable training plan: the ordered sequence of workouts — each targeting a specific physiological determinant, each with its paces stored relative to the current state estimate — arranged into a periodized structure that peaks the athlete on race day. It is the system's **plan generator**: a procedure that, given the current state model, the goal contract, and the athlete's scheduling constraints, emits a plan the device can execute and the downstream loops can adapt. It conforms to `research/00` as the decision authority — the supreme objective of expected average race pace (`research/00` PRIN-01), relative anchoring of every pace to the current state estimate (`research/00` ARCH-04), the intensity-distribution register row (`research/00` REG-07), the individualization governing rule (`research/00` IND-01), the confidence-widens-guardrails finding (`research/00` ARCH-11), and the plan-versus-goal ownership boundary (`research/00` GOAL-01) — and cites `research/04` §2–§3 for periodization structure, the intensity-distribution models, and the workout typology, and `research/01` §1–§2, §4, §6 for the determinant-to-stimulus mapping the plan is built on. Per the project conventions, every rule is stated here in full; the evidence behind it lives in the cited docs and is not re-argued. Every numeric constant below is a **heuristic default** (`research/00` DOC-06), tunable per athlete only under the individualization rule (`research/00` IND-01) and never where it is a design invariant.*

## 5.1 What this section owns, and the boundaries it respects

Section 5 owns the **generation of the training plan**: the workout data representation every later section reads and writes, the workout typology, the periodization structure, the intensity distribution, the relative-anchored pace prescription, and the logic that decides which physiological determinant a given block of training should target. It is the discharge of the project's build requirement to "generate a training plan made of detailed, prescriptive workouts, each targeting specific physiological determinants, with recovery and periodization built in." Four boundaries fix what this section is and is not.

- **Section 5 generates the plan; Section 6 adapts it.** This section is a **generator** — conceptually a pure function from `(state model, goal contract, athlete constraints, calendar) → plan`. It produces the plan the athlete starts with, and it is re-invoked whenever a material change warrants a fresh plan (a large state-model shift, a goal-contract change, a schedule disruption). It does **not** own the day-to-day adaptation that reshapes the plan in flight — the day-of readiness gate, the recent-workout re-anchoring, the ramp-rate and monotony guardrails, the injury-risk override, and the decision of *when* to re-periodize. Those are Section 6's, which reads the same state model and calls this generator when it decides a re-plan is due (`research/00` ARB-01, the arbitration ladder; `research/05` §5). The division is deliberate: Section 5 answers "what is the right plan given what we know now," Section 6 answers "given today's signals, what does the athlete actually do, and has enough changed to warrant a new plan."
- **Section 5 places and shapes the taper only at the skeleton level; Section 7 owns its depth and individualization.** The periodization structure this section defines (§5.5) includes the taper as the terminal mesocycle and fixes where it sits and what it is for, and it includes the recovery-week cadence as a slot in the mesocycle rhythm. The *detailed* taper — its exact length and volume-cut depth, its exponential decay shape, how it is personalized by chronic load and race distance, and how it is verified against the projected form (TSB) trajectory — is Section 7's, and the mandatory recovery-week guardrails are shared with Section 6/Section 7. Section 5 leaves those blocks as well-defined slots with default shapes and defers their controllers downstream.
- **Section 5 consumes the state estimate; it does not compute it.** The anchors every prescribed pace is stored against — functional threshold pace, critical speed, vVO₂max — and the determinant profile the plan targets are Section 4's, read here as inputs (§4.2, §4.7). Section 5 never re-estimates a determinant; it reads the current estimate and its confidence and prescribes against them. Likewise the gap-to-goal projection that tells the plan how far the athlete is from the goal pace is Section 1's pace-target procedure (§1.4), which Section 5 calls; Section 5 owns the *decomposition* of that gap by determinant and the choice of which determinant to target (§5.7), not the projection itself.
- **Section 5 owns the outbound structured-workout representation; Section 2 owns the channel.** Section 2 (§2.7.1) identified the Garmin **Training API** as the outbound channel that publishes structured workouts and plans to the athlete's Connect calendar for step-by-step execution on the watch, but deliberately left the representation undefined. Section 5 defines that representation (§5.2) and the mapping onto the Training API's structured-workout format (§5.8); the channel, its authentication, and its post-session counterpart remain Section 2's.

Two upstream sections read parameters this section owns, and Section 5 defines them here so their forward references close: Section 3's durability metric (§3.8) reads the **qualifying-long-run** definition, and Section 4's state model (§4.4.1, §4.7) reads the **maximal-effort** definition to decide which sessions count as critical-speed, vVO₂max, threshold, and durability observations. Both are workout-typology parameters and are specified in §5.2.6.

## 5.2 The workout data representation

Every workout in the system — prescribed, executed, or adapted — is one instance of a single machine-representable structure, so that the generator, the device push (§5.8), the completion-vs-prescription comparison Section 3 records (§3.2), and the adaptation logic (Section 6) all reason over workouts uniformly rather than special-casing each type (`research/04` §3, "Design notes for a machine representation"). The structure is deliberately small: a workout reduces to an ordered list of **segments** plus **metadata**, and that shape spans the entire typology of §5.3.

### 5.2.1 The segment as the atom

A workout is an **ordered list of segments**. Each segment is one continuous block of running (or of recovery between running blocks) with a single intensity intent. A segment carries:

| Field | Type | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| `role` | enum | `warmup`, `work`, `recovery`, `rest`, `cooldown`. Distinguishes the physiologically-loaded portion (`work`) from the surrounding structure, so load accounting and completion scoring weight the right parts. |
| `intensity` | intensity spec | The target intensity, expressed relative to a state anchor (§5.2.2). |
| `quantity` | duration/distance spec | How long the segment lasts (§5.2.3). |
| `repeat` | integer | For interval sets: how many times the enclosed segment(s) repeat (§5.2.4). Default 1. |
| `children` | list of segments | For set structure: a segment may contain sub-segments (a rep + its recovery), enabling sets-of-reps nesting (§5.2.4). |
| `cue` | string / enum | Optional execution cue for the athlete/device (e.g. "relaxed," "control the downhills," "last one fastest"). |

This recursive segment/children shape is what lets one structure express both a flat continuous run (one `work` segment) and a nested set (e.g. 2 sets of [5 × (3 min work + 90 s recovery)] with a 3 min set-recovery) without any type-specific representation.

### 5.2.2 Intensity specification and relative anchoring

A segment's `intensity` is **never a stored absolute pace**. It is a reference to a **state anchor** plus a value or range against that anchor, so that when Section 4 updates the state estimate, every prescribed pace updates automatically and no plan ever carries a stale hard-coded pace (`research/00` ARCH-04; `research/04` §3). An intensity spec carries:

| Sub-field | Type | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| `anchor_type` | enum | Which state anchor the target is relative to: `threshold_pace`, `critical_speed`, `vvo2max`, `goal_race_pace`, or the secondary anchors `heart_rate` (as %HRR or a zone) and `rpe` (0–10). |
| `value` | number or range | The target relative to the anchor — e.g. `1.00 × threshold_pace` for a tempo, `0.90 × threshold_pace` (i.e. faster) for VO₂max work expressed as a fraction, or a `[low, high]` band. Ranges are the norm, not points, because pace floats with terrain and conditions. |
| `resolved_target` | derived, transient | The absolute pace/HR the relative spec resolves to **at execution time**, computed from the current anchor value. Not stored on the plan; recomputed whenever the anchor changes or the workout is pushed to the device. |

The primary anchors are pace/velocity anchors from the state model — `threshold_pace` and `critical_speed` above all, with `vvo2max` for the shortest, fastest work — because they are the determinants Section 4 estimates and re-anchors from the athlete's own efforts (§4.2), and `goal_race_pace` (GRP), which is the goal contract's pace target (§1.2, §1.4) used for race-specific work. Storing every target relative to these anchors is what makes the plan a live object: a confirmed threshold improvement in Section 4 instantly and consistently shifts every threshold-anchored and zone-derived pace across the whole remaining plan, with no plan rewrite.

### 5.2.3 Duration and quantity specification

A segment's `quantity` is one of three kinds, matching how the typology (§5.3) actually prescribes work:

- **Time** — a duration in seconds (e.g. a 30 min tempo, a 3 min VO₂max rep). The default for most quality work, because it is terrain-independent.
- **Distance** — metres (e.g. 1000 m reps, a 32 km long run). Used where the athlete or the workout convention is distance-native.
- **Open-ended** — terminated by a condition rather than a fixed amount. Two conditions are supported: a **lap/manual** trigger (the athlete presses lap), and a **performance-based cutoff** — continue until pace at the target effort can no longer be held within a stated tolerance, or HR-at-pace drifts beyond a bound (`research/04` §5.3, the velocity-based-training analogue). The performance-based cutoff is the representation an optional in-session module (Section 2 §2.7.4) or a post-hoc completion analysis uses to make a rep count state-dependent rather than fixed; where no live module executes it, the plan carries the fixed-count form and the cutoff is evaluated post-session as a completion feature. It is flagged as a principled extrapolation from resistance-training evidence, per `research/04` §5.3.

### 5.2.4 Interval sets and structure

Interval work is expressed with `repeat` and `children`. A simple set is a `work` segment with a `recovery` child, repeated: `repeat: 5, children: [work(3 min @ vVO₂max), recovery(2 min @ easy)]`. Sets-of-reps nest one level further: an outer segment with `repeat: 2` whose children are the inner repeated set plus a set-recovery segment. The recovery spec is a full intensity+quantity segment in its own right (its own anchor, value, and duration/distance), because recovery intensity and length are themselves prescription parameters that matter physiologically — VO₂max intervals with jog recovery versus standing recovery are different stimuli (`research/04` §3). This structure spans the whole catalog of §5.3 with no special cases and lets the adaptation logic scale a set (change `repeat`, or the rep `quantity`) or swap a continuous tempo for cruise intervals by editing the segment list, not by branching on workout type.

### 5.2.5 Workout metadata: target, phase, and the completion contract

Beyond its segments, each workout carries metadata that the generator sets and the downstream loops read:

| Field | Type | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| `workout_type` | enum | The typology archetype (§5.3): `recovery`, `easy`, `long_run` (with sub-type), `tempo`, `cruise_intervals`, `vo2max_intervals`, `anaerobic_reps`, `hill_repeats`, `strides`, `race_pace`. |
| `primary_target` | enum | The physiological target this workout is chosen to develop (§5.7.3): `vo2max`, `threshold_cs`, `economy`, `durability`, `anaerobic`, `neuromuscular`, or `aerobic_base`. It ties a workout to a Section 4 determinant where one exists (`vo2max`, `threshold_cs`, `economy`, `durability`); `aerobic_base` and `neuromuscular` map to the volume/structure and supporting-work channels of §5.7.3, which Section 4 does not carry as standalone determinants. |
| `phase` | enum | The periodization phase this workout belongs to: `base`, `build`, `peak`, `taper` (§5.5). |
| `planned_date` | date | When the plan schedules it; the microcycle placement (§5.5.3). |
| `target_bands` | derived | Per `work` segment, the acceptable pace/HR band around the resolved target, against which Section 3 scores completion-vs-prescription (§3.2). Section 5 owns these bands; Section 3 records only the measured-vs-target comparison. |
| `recovery_cost_tag` | enum | The session-type recovery time-course tag (easy ≈ 1 day; threshold ≈ 24–48 h; VO₂max ≈ 48–72 h; long run ≈ 48–72 h+), set from `workout_type` per `research/01` §6.3. Section 5 sets it on the *prescribed* workout so the microcycle spacing (§5.5.3) can respect it before execution; on ingestion of the *executed* session, Section 3 §3.5.5 carries the same tag forward (re-deriving it from the same `workout_type`→§6.3 mapping) and attaches the residual-fatigue decay clock, which Section 6 then acts on. Both derive from one mapping, so the prescribed and executed tags do not diverge. |

The **completion contract** is the pairing of `target_bands` with the executed session: when a prescribed workout is later ingested (Section 2) and features are extracted (Section 3 §3.2), each `work` segment's measured grade-adjusted pace and HR are compared against the band Section 5 set, and the shortfall/overshoot is recorded as a feature. This is the closed-loop signal that tells the system whether the plan is calibrated to the athlete — systematic overshooting of effort to hit paces (HR higher than expected for the target) or undershooting of paces flags that the state estimate or the plan is mis-calibrated and is a primary trigger for Section 4 re-anchoring and Section 6 re-planning (`research/04` §5.4, completion-vs-prescription).

### 5.2.6 The two typology parameters upstream sections read

Two definitions are workout-typology parameters that this section owns and that upstream sections read; they are stated here so their forward references (Section 3 §3.8, Section 4 §4.4.1/§4.7) close cleanly.

**Qualifying long run (for the durability metric).** Section 3's durability feature (§3.8) is computed only on runs long and steady enough to expose durability. Section 5 defines a **qualifying long run** as a predominantly steady-aerobic run whose duration reaches the qualifying threshold — shipped default **≥ 90 minutes** of running (or, distance-natively, a run at or above the athlete's long-run distance band for the goal event), run predominantly in Zone 1 with no more than incidental time above it, so the back-third EF/decoupling drift reflects accumulated fatigue rather than an embedded hard effort. A run with a large embedded quality block (a marathon-pace or progression long run, §5.3) still qualifies for durability read-out on its steady portions, but the drift is computed on grade-adjusted pace over comparable-effort thirds, excluding the deliberately-hard segment, so the fast finish is not mistaken for a durability failure. The 90 min default is a heuristic, tunable per athlete only under the individualization rule (`research/00` IND-01) and scaled by goal distance (shorter for a 10K athlete, longer for a marathoner).

**Qualifying maximal effort (for the state-model fits).** Section 4 fits critical speed and D′, vVO₂max, functional threshold pace, and the individual endurance exponent from the athlete's genuinely maximal or near-maximal efforts (§4.2, §4.4.1). Section 5 defines what counts as a **qualifying maximal effort** and tags it so Section 4 can identify it: an effort is a qualifying maximal effort when it is a **race** (`activity_tag = race`), a **time trial**, or a **workout `work` segment executed at or beyond its target to volitional limit** — operationally, a sustained continuous effort of trustworthy stream quality (§2.4) whose grade-adjusted pace meets or exceeds the resolved target for its duration and which the completion features show was sustained to the end of the segment rather than faded, over a duration that falls in one of the state-model's fitting bands (roughly 2–20 min for critical speed, 3–8 min for vVO₂max, 20–60 min for threshold; `research/05` §3.1). The tag records the effort's duration and grade-adjusted distance so Section 4 can place it in the correct fitting band, and it records whether the effort was a true race/time trial (highest quality, small measurement variance) or a workout segment (lower quality). Section 5 owns the tagging rule; Section 4 owns the fits and the confidence it assigns each. This is also the mechanism by which the generator deliberately *creates* observations: scheduling a time trial or a tune-up race when the state model's critical-speed or threshold confidence has decayed (§4.3, staleness) is Section 5 supplying Section 4 the fresh maximal effort it needs to re-anchor.

## 5.3 The workout typology

The generator draws from a fixed catalog of workout archetypes, each with a physiological target, the parameters needed to prescribe it, and its periodization home (`research/04` §3; physiological targets per `research/01` §1–§2). Every archetype is expressed in the §5.2 representation; the catalog below is the set of templates the generator instantiates and then anchors (§5.4) and schedules (§5.5). Intensities are named relative to the state anchors, not as absolute paces.

| Workout type | Primary determinant target | Key prescription parameters | Periodization home |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Recovery run** | Aerobic maintenance, blood flow | 20–40 min; well below Zone 1 top (RPE 2–3) | All phases, after hard days |
| **Easy / general aerobic** | Aerobic base (mitochondria, capillarity, fat oxidation, durability substrate) | 40–90 min; Zone 1 pace/HR; optional strides appended | All phases; the bulk of volume |
| **Long run** | Peripheral endurance, glycogen economy, durability | Duration/distance (often 90–150 min, or ~20–35% of weekly volume); Zone 1 | All phases; weekly anchor |
| • Progression long run | Durability + late-run pacing under fatigue | Total duration; final segment stepped from easy → threshold/GRP | Build/peak |
| • Fast-finish long run | Running fast on tired legs; race simulation | Easy body + final 15–40 min at ~GRP or threshold | Build/peak |
| • Marathon-pace long run | Marathon-specific efficiency, fueling, pacing | Total duration; embedded or continuous block at GRP/MP | Marathon peak |
| **Tempo / threshold (continuous)** | Threshold / critical speed | 20–40 min continuous at threshold pace | Base → build support |
| **Cruise intervals (broken threshold)** | Threshold / critical speed (more volume at controlled effort) | Reps × 5–10 min at threshold; short (~1 min) jog recovery | Base → build |
| **VO₂max intervals** | VO₂max / vVO₂max | Reps × 2–5 min (or ~800–1200 m) at ~95–100% vVO₂max / ~3–5K effort; recovery ≈ 50–100% of rep duration | Build/peak |
| **Anaerobic / speed reps** | Anaerobic capacity, buffering, neuromuscular power | Short reps (200–400 m or 30–90 s) faster than vVO₂max; full recovery | Peak; more for 5K/10K |
| **Hill repeats** | Strength/power, economy, VO₂ stimulus (lower impact) | Reps × 30 s–4 min uphill hard; jog-down recovery; grade specified | Base (strength) → build (power) |
| **Strides** | Neuromuscular sharpening, economy | 4–8 × 15–25 s at ~mile–3K effort, relaxed; full recovery | All phases, appended to easy runs |
| **Race-pace work** | Specificity: efficiency and pacing at exact GRP | Volume at GRP, continuous or long intervals | Peak / sharpening |

Two things about this catalog are load-bearing for the rest of the section. First, the `primary_target` column is what connects a workout to a determinant in the state model, so the determinant-targeting logic (§5.7) is a matter of selecting and dosing archetypes by their target column, not of inventing new session types. Second, running economy and durability have **no single dedicated "economy workout" or "durability workout"** in the classical sense — economy is built by accumulated easy volume over months, plus strength/plyometric work and the neuromuscular stimulus of strides, hills, and fast reps, and durability is built by long-run volume and progressively longer race-pace efforts (`research/01` §2.3–§2.4). The generator therefore develops these two determinants through the *volume and long-run structure* of the plan and the supporting strides/hills, not through a named quality session; §5.7.3 makes this mapping explicit. Strength and plyometric training, which `research/01` §2.3 identifies as one of the better-supported economy stimuli, is represented as a non-running supporting-work slot the plan can schedule, but its detailed prescription is outside the running-workout typology and is carried as a flagged recommendation rather than a device-executed running workout.

## 5.4 Relative-anchored pace prescription and the zone system

Every pace the plan prescribes is derived from the state model's anchors, so the plan is a function of the current state estimate and updates with it (`research/00` ARCH-04). This subsection defines the anchors it reads, the zone system it builds from them, and the automatic re-anchoring behavior.

### 5.4.1 The anchors from Section 4

The generator reads, from the current state model (§4.2, §4.7), the anchors and their confidences: **functional threshold pace** (the LT2/critical-intensity anchor, the workhorse of the zone system), **critical speed and D′** (the sustainable-pace asymptote and the finite above-CS capacity), **vVO₂max** (the anchor for VO₂max-interval and short severe work), and — from the goal contract via Section 1's pace-target procedure (§1.4) — **goal race pace**. Each anchor carries a confidence; the generator reads it and lets low confidence widen its prescriptions and damp its ambition (§5.7.2, per `research/00` ARCH-11 and §4.6). Heart rate (as a %HRR or a zone, computed from the state model's HR_rest/HR_max, §4.2.7) and RPE (a 0–10 subjective effort target, not derived from the HR anchors) are secondary anchors, used as described in §5.4.4.

### 5.4.2 The zone system derived from the anchors

The system prescribes against the three-domain intensity model anchored at the two thresholds (`research/01` §3.1; `research/04` §2): Zone 1 (moderate, below the aerobic threshold LT1), Zone 2 (heavy, LT1→LT2, the tempo/threshold band), and Zone 3 (severe, above LT2/critical speed up to VO₂max). The upper boundary of Zone 2 / lower boundary of Zone 3 **is** the functional threshold pace / critical speed the state model owns, so that boundary is directly anchored. Finer 5-zone prescription convenience is supported as a subdivision of these three domains but rests on the same two physiological anchors plus vVO₂max at the top (`research/01` §3.1).

The **aerobic-threshold (LT1) boundary that separates Zone 1 from Zone 2 is not an independent determinant Section 4 publishes**, so the generator derives it from the threshold anchor by a fixed relationship: LT1 is placed at a **fixed fraction of functional threshold velocity** (shipped default: the Zone-1 ceiling at roughly the pace the athlete could sustain well below threshold — a default in the region of ~80–88% of threshold *velocity*, i.e. a controlled easy-to-steady pace). In v1 the boundary therefore moves only with the threshold anchor, never independently of it (`research/00` LT1-01). **This LT1-from-threshold derivation is a spec-introduced default under uncertainty**: `research/01` §3.1 gives the two-anchor physiological model but no research doc states the LT1/LT2 ratio to use, and the true ratio varies between athletes. The default is transparent and tunable, it degrades safely (an easy run prescribed slightly too easy costs little, and the ~80%-easy volume target of §5.6 is robust to the exact boundary), and it is flagged and logged as an open item. **Disposition (`research/00` LT1-01, LT1-02; 2026-08-31):** the fraction-of-threshold default **stands for v1**; the recommended future path is to promote LT1 to its own estimated determinant, with DFA-α1 = 0.75 the leading candidate, deferred on RR-quality/validation grounds and recorded in `research/00` LT1-02 and `future/future-directions.md`. Moving the boundary toward an LT1 surrogate in the athlete's own data is not v1 behaviour: it belongs to that future determinant, and `future/future-directions.md` (the LT1 entry) records it with its preconditions — the deferral of the DFA-α1 determinant past v1 and the explicit amendment of the in-activity-HRV prohibition it requires first (`research/00` HRV-05, LT1-04).

### 5.4.3 Relative storage and automatic re-anchoring

Because every `intensity` spec stores an `anchor_type` and a relative `value` rather than an absolute pace (§5.2.2), the plan re-anchors itself with no rewrite whenever Section 4 updates an anchor. When the state model publishes a new threshold pace (Section 4 re-anchors it continuously, §4.2.3, §4.5), the generator does not edit the plan; the `resolved_target` of every threshold-anchored and zone-derived segment simply recomputes against the new value at execution/push time. This is the operational payoff of relative anchoring and the reason a stale threshold cannot silently corrupt the plan the way it would corrupt an absolute-pace plan. The individualization rule governs which anchors are trusted to re-anchor continuously versus held near a default: threshold pace and its derived zones re-anchor continuously; critical speed, vVO₂max, and the endurance exponent re-anchor once enough qualifying maximal efforts (§5.2.6) identify them (`research/00` IND-02, IND-05; §4.5). The generator never prescribes off an anchor at higher precision than the anchor's confidence supports — a low-confidence anchor yields wider target bands (§5.2.5) and more conservative ambition (§5.7.2), not a falsely precise pace.

### 5.4.4 Secondary anchors and when they take over

Pace decouples from effort on hills, in heat, and at altitude, so heart rate and RPE are carried as secondary anchors and take precedence in exactly the conditions where pace misleads (`research/04` §3). The generator attaches an HR band or an RPE target alongside the pace target on any segment likely to be run in decoupling conditions, and the execution rule is: **on graded, hot, or high-altitude sessions, hold effort (HR/RPE) and let pace float**, rather than chasing a pace the conditions make unsafe or unrepresentative. Grade-adjusted pace (Section 3 §3.3) reconciles the executed session back to a flat-equivalent for load and completion scoring, so an effort-anchored hill session still scores correctly against its target band. This keeps the pace prescription primary where pace is trustworthy (flat, temperate) and effort primary where it is not.

## 5.5 Periodization structure

The plan is organized as a periodized progression that sequences adaptations so the trainable determinants peak on race day (`research/04` §1). The structure is standard in vocabulary and physiological logic; the system's contribution is that it is generated from, and continuously re-anchored to, the athlete's own state model.

### 5.5.1 The macrocycle: base → build → peak → taper

The **macrocycle** is the full run-up from program start to `race_date` (§1.2), typically 12–24 weeks for the tuning-target amateur (`research/04` §1). It is divided into four phase blocks whose ordering follows the general-to-specific and trainability principles — earliest emphasis on the adaptations that are slowest, most foundational, and least race-specific; latest emphasis on those that are fast, fatiguing, and most race-specific (`research/04` §1; `research/01` §2):

- **Base (general preparation).** Build the aerobic and musculoskeletal infrastructure — mitochondrial density, capillarity, stroke/plasma volume, fat oxidation, and the durability substrate — chiefly through a high volume of Zone-1 running plus supporting strides and hills. These adaptations are slow and durable, so they come first and are trained longest (`research/04` §1; `research/01` §2.1–§2.4).
- **Build (specific preparation).** Layer threshold/critical-speed and VO₂max stimuli onto the base to raise the metabolic ceiling, once the base can support the added fatigue (`research/04` §1).
- **Peak / race-specific sharpening.** Shift the highest-quality work toward goal race pace and effort, integrating the developed capacities into the specific race demand — the specificity endpoint (`research/04` §1, the Canova illustration).
- **Taper.** Shed accumulated fatigue while preserving fitness so form peaks on race day. Section 5 places the taper as the terminal block (default ~2 weeks; `research/00` REG-08, the taper row) and leaves its depth, shape, and individualization to Section 7.

The phase-length allocation is a function of total weeks-to-race, goal distance, and training age (§5.5.4). Because `research/04` §1 fixes the *ordering* of the blocks but supplies no split, the generator ships a **spec-introduced default allocation**, flagged under the uncertainty convention and tunable: the taper takes the terminal ~2 weeks (`research/00` REG-08, the taper row; Section 7 owns its depth), and the remaining weeks are divided **base ≈ 50%, build ≈ 30%, peak ≈ 20%** as the baseline for the tuning-target amateur, shifted by goal distance and training age per §5.5.4 (a marathon build carries proportionally more base and long-run weeks; a 5K/10K build shifts weeks toward build/peak intensity). When the calendar is short, the generator **compresses the base first**, preserving the ordering and a minimum viable peak-plus-taper (default floor ~3–4 combined weeks) so specificity and peaking are never squeezed out; when it is long, the extra weeks extend the base. Recovery weeks (§5.5.2) are embedded within these blocks, not added on top. **This split has been ratified and back-ported to the `research/00` register, REG-14 (2026-08-31);** it remains a heuristic default, tunable per athlete only under the individualization rule (`research/00` IND-01), and is flagged in `future/future-directions.md` as a human-coach-specification candidate.

### 5.5.2 Mesocycles and the general-to-specific logic

Within each phase, training is organized into **mesocycles** — multi-week blocks (commonly 3–5 weeks) each with a dominant physiological aim (`research/04` §1). The default mesocycle rhythm is a progressive-overload build across the loading weeks followed by a **recovery week** (a planned reduction in volume/intensity to consolidate adaptation and manage fatigue). Section 5 places the recovery-week slot in the rhythm (shipped default: every 4th week, tunable by training age and observed recovery) and defers the mandatory recovery-week guardrails and their depth to Section 6/Section 7. The system **concentrates a small number of compatible targets per block, and concentrates them more tightly as training age rises** (`research/04` §1, block periodization), rather than developing everything at once; the choice of which target each mesocycle concentrates on is the determinant-targeting logic of §5.7. (The phase-driven pyramidal→polarizing shift in intensity distribution is a separate axis, owned by §5.6, not a function of training age.) The evidence does not crown one periodization scheme universally superior, so the defensible default the system ships is systematic progressive overload with undulating (hard/easy) microcycles inside broadly sequenced phases, with block concentration increasing with training age (`research/04` §1).

### 5.5.3 The microcycle and hard/easy spacing

The **microcycle** is the recurring short unit, almost universally one week, in which sessions of differing purpose and intensity are arranged so hard stimuli are separated by adequate recovery (`research/04` §1). The generator builds each week from a template that honors two constraints: the **intensity distribution** target for the current phase (§5.6), and the **recovery time-courses by session type** (`research/01` §6.3) — easy aerobic recovers within ~a day and can be frequent; threshold needs ~24–48 h; VO₂max/severe intervals need ~48–72 h; long runs need ~48–72 h+. Hard sessions are therefore spaced by their `recovery_cost_tag` (§5.2.5) so that two high-cost sessions are not scheduled inside each other's recovery window, easy/recovery days fill the gaps, and the long run anchors the week. This hard-easy alternation and the ~7-day cycle are the direct consequence of the recovery time-courses, not an independent convention (`research/01` §6.3; `research/04` §1). The generator produces the *planned* microcycle; Section 6's day-of readiness gate may still down-regulate any given hard day, and the arbitration ladder (`research/00` ARB-01) governs that — but placement of hard sessions stays with the plan, consistent with the down-regulate-freely / up-regulate-cautiously asymmetry (`research/00` PRIN-13): the daily gate may soften a planned hard day but never manufacture one.

### 5.5.4 Parameterization by distance, training age, and goal-race conditions

The structure above is parameterized, not fixed, by the goal distance and an estimate of the athlete's training age/fitness (`research/04` §1). The shorter and faster the race, the larger the role of VO₂max, anaerobic capacity, and neuromuscular speed, so 5K/10K generation carries more VO₂max intervals, faster race-pace work, and speed development, with a shorter taper. The longer the race, the more it is limited by fractional utilization, economy, and durability, so half-marathon and especially marathon generation emphasizes threshold volume, long runs, marathon-pace-specific work, and fueling practice, with a somewhat longer taper (`research/04` §1; `research/01` Table 1, §1.5–§1.6). Training age shifts the defaults too: novices adapt to almost any progressive stimulus and need less specialized structure and lower absolute loads; experienced athletes have smaller adaptive windows, need higher and more specific stimuli, and benefit more from concentrated block loading and careful fatigue management (`research/04` §1). Training age is read from the state model and history (the depth and span of qualifying efforts, the established chronic load); where it is uncertain, the generator defaults conservatively (lower volume, more general structure) and lets the plan progress as the athlete's data accrues, consistent with the low-confidence-widens-guardrails posture (`research/00` ARCH-11).

Finally, the **goal race's expected conditions** parameterize block emphasis as well. A hot/humid or high-altitude target, or a markedly hilly course (the environmental and course modifiers Section 1 §1.4.4 reads), raises the training value of the determinants that best protect pace under that specific stress — durability, running economy, and threshold — so the generator shifts block emphasis toward them through the determinant-addressability weighting (§5.7.2), even though the environmental modifiers themselves are pacing-strategy inputs and not trainable determinants (§5.7.1). This is emphasis-shaping only: the conservative no-acclimatization default for the pace target still holds (Section 1 §1.4.4; Section 6 §6.9), and a dedicated heat/altitude acclimation block is a deferred future addendum (Section 6 §6.9; `future/future-directions.md`), not part of v1 generation.

## 5.6 Intensity distribution

How the plan's volume is distributed across the three intensity zones follows the intensity-distribution register row exactly (`research/00` REG-07, REG-22; `research/04` §2; `research/01` §3.3): the plan is **pyramidal-leaning in base and polarizing through build and peak**, holds **Zone-1 volume near ~80% of training time throughout**, and treats the **split of the remaining ~20% between Zone 2 (threshold) and Zone 3 (high-intensity) as a phase- and distance-dependent tunable parameter** rather than a fixed constant. Concretely:

- The ~80% easy / ~20% hard proportion is measured **by time (or session volume), not by session count**, and is held roughly constant across the macrocycle — the large majority of running is easy in every phase, which is the robustly established finding (`research/04` §2; `research/01` §3.3).
- In the **base** phase the hard ~20% is distributed **pyramidally** — more of it at threshold (Zone 2) and little truly high-intensity (Zone 3) — matching the higher-volume, longer-event, foundation-building emphasis (`research/04` §2).
- Through **build and peak** the distribution **polarizes** — the same easy base, but the quality work sharpens toward Zone 3 VO₂max and race-pace intervals, with less time in the threshold middle (`research/04` §2).
- Pure **threshold-dominant** distributions (a large fraction of time in the Zone-2 "grey zone") are avoided as the least-supported pattern and the one carrying the highest chronic-fatigue risk (`research/04` §2; `research/01` §3.3).

The Z2/Z3 split is exposed as a tunable parameter because the polarized-versus-pyramidal superiority question is genuinely unsettled in the literature (`research/04` §2; `research/01` §3.3); the system ships the phase-and-distance-dependent default above and states it as an assumption rather than a proven optimum, per the uncertainty-explicit convention. Shorter events push the build/peak split further toward Z3; longer events keep more of the quality at threshold even in the peak. Monotony is guarded structurally by the hard/easy microcycle (§5.5.3) and monitored by Section 3's monotony/strain signals (§3.9), which Section 6 reads to inject variation when a week drifts toward samey daily loads.

## 5.7 Determinant-targeted plan generation

The generator does not produce a generic plan and hope it helps; it decides which physiological determinant to concentrate on, block by block, by asking which determinant — if improved — would close the most of the athlete's gap to the goal pace, subject to what is actually trainable in the time available and at the confidence available. This is the step that ties the plan to the success metric.

### 5.7.1 The gap-to-goal decomposition

The starting point is Section 1's pace-target procedure (§1.4), which projects the athlete's current expected average race pace from the state model, and the goal contract's `goal_pace_target` (§1.2). The difference between them is the **gap to goal**. Because Section 1's projection is built as an explicit chain — CS/vVO₂max anchor → endurance-exponent projection to the race distance → economy and durability adjustments → course/environment modifiers (§1.4.1–§1.4.5) — the gap can be **decomposed by determinant**: the generator asks, for each trainable determinant (critical speed / threshold, vVO₂max, economy, durability, and the endurance exponent), how much the projected race pace would improve if that determinant moved by a plausible training-driven increment while the others held. The determinant whose plausible improvement closes the most gap is the **largest addressable gap-to-goal**, and it is the candidate the block should target (`research/05` §4; the decomposition the Section 4 determinant profile enables, §4.7). The course/environment modifiers (§1.4.4) are not trainable determinants and are excluded from the decomposition — they shape pacing strategy, not the training target — though a hot or hilly goal race raises the value of the determinants (durability, economy, threshold) that most protect pace under that specific stress, which the generator reflects in the goal-race-condition parameterization (§5.5.4).

### 5.7.2 Addressability: trainability, time-to-race, and confidence

The largest *raw* gap contributor is not automatically the right target, because determinants differ in how trainable they are, how fast they respond, and how confidently the system knows them. The generator weights each determinant's gap contribution by three factors before choosing (`research/01` §2; `research/00` REG-13, IND-01, ARCH-11):

- **Trainability / ceiling.** For the trained tuning-target amateur, threshold/critical speed, economy, and durability have high, durable headroom, while VO₂max has a smaller, lower ceiling that plateaus in trained runners even as threshold and economy keep improving (`research/01` §2.1–§2.4). A large VO₂max gap in an already-trained athlete is only partly addressable; the same-sized threshold or economy gap is more so.
- **Time-to-race.** Adaptations have different time courses — VO₂max responds in ~3 weeks, threshold over weeks-to-months, economy and durability over months (`research/01` §2). With many weeks to race, the slow, high-ceiling determinants (economy, durability, threshold) are worth targeting; with few weeks, the plan favors the faster-responding determinants and race-specific sharpening, because there is not time to move economy. The generator reads weeks-to-race from `race_date` and the current date and shifts the target accordingly.
- **Confidence.** A determinant known at low confidence (§4.3, §4.6) is weaker evidence for aggressive up-regulation: the system may always target the aerobic base and reduce load safely, but it does not raise ambition — faster prescribed paces, bigger volume, harder key sessions — off a low-confidence determinant estimate (`research/00` PRIN-13, ARCH-11; §4.6). A confident threshold rise justifies moving the athlete's paces and concentrating a block on pushing it further; a shaky estimate does not, and the block defaults to the safe, always-valuable aerobic-base and volume work while the system gathers the maximal efforts (§5.2.6) that would raise confidence.

The product of (gap contribution × trainability × time-availability × confidence) is the determinant's **addressability score**, and the generator concentrates each mesocycle on the highest-scoring addressable determinant, re-evaluating as the state model updates and the race nears (so a base block might target economy/durability via volume, a build block the threshold, and the peak race-specific sharpening regardless of the raw gap, because specificity dominates as the race approaches). **The addressability scoring — combining the four factors into one ranking — is a spec-introduced construction**: `research/01` §2 and `research/04` §1 supply the ingredients (trainability, ceilings, time courses, the general-to-specific principle) but not a formula for combining them, so the multiplicative scoring above is a transparent engineering default. **It has been ratified and back-ported to the `research/00` register, REG-13 (2026-08-31)** and is flagged in `future/future-directions.md` as a human-coach-specification candidate; it remains inspectable and degrades safely: when the factors are uncertain, the score collapses toward the always-valid default of aerobic base plus phase-appropriate specificity.

### 5.7.3 Determinant-to-stimulus mapping

Once a block's target determinant is chosen, the generator develops it with the stimulus the physiology research assigns to it (`research/01` §2; `research/04` §3). The mapping:

- **VO₂max** → VO₂max intervals (reps of ~2–5 min at ~95–100% vVO₂max) as the primary stimulus, supported by sustained high-volume aerobic running which raises stroke volume; hill repeats supply a VO₂ stimulus at lower impact (`research/01` §2.1; `research/04` §3). Central-dominant, fast-responding, low ceiling in the trained athlete — targeted more for shorter events and when the race is near.
- **Threshold / critical speed** → continuous tempo and cruise intervals at threshold pace, on top of a large aerobic volume; peripheral-dominant, high and durable trainability, the primary long-term lever for the serious amateur (`research/01` §2.2; `research/04` §3). The workhorse target across base and build.
- **Running economy** → accumulated easy aerobic volume over the whole macrocycle (the strongest correlate of good economy), plus the neuromuscular stimuli of strides, hill repeats, and — as a flagged supporting-work recommendation — heavy/explosive strength and plyometric training; slow to respond but high headroom, especially for amateurs who have not maximized it (`research/01` §2.3). Developed structurally through the plan's volume and supporting work, not a named quality session (§5.3).
- **Durability** → long-run volume and progressively longer race-pace efforts (progression, fast-finish, and marathon-pace long runs), which repeatedly expose the muscle to prolonged substrate use and fatigue; emerging construct, plausibly volume-driven, read out by the late-run decoupling Section 3 §3.8 trends (`research/01` §1.6, §2.4). Weighted heavily for the marathon, lightly for the 5K (§5.5.4).
- **Endurance exponent (fractional utilization)** → not targeted directly; it is flattened by the same durability and long-run-volume work above (`research/01` §4.3), so improving durability and the long run improves the exponent, and the generator treats a poor exponent as a durability/volume signal rather than a separate target.
- **Anaerobic / neuromuscular** → short fast reps and strides; targeted mainly for 5K/10K sharpening in the peak (`research/01` §1, §2; `research/04` §3).

Race-pace work sits above this mapping in the peak phase regardless of the raw gap decomposition: as the race nears, specificity — efficiency and pacing at exactly the goal pace and effort — is the dominant stimulus, integrating whatever capacities the earlier blocks built into the specific race demand (`research/04` §1, the specificity endpoint). The generator therefore always converges the peak on goal-race-pace work, using the gap decomposition to choose the *earlier* blocks' emphasis rather than to override the specificity principle at the end.

## 5.8 The plan-generation procedure and the outbound push

Putting the pieces together, the generator runs as a defined procedure and emits a plan in the §5.2 representation, ready for the outbound channel.

1. **Read inputs.** The current state model with confidences (Section 4), the goal contract and its pace target and gap-to-goal (Section 1 §1.4, §1.2), weeks-to-race from `race_date`, the athlete's scheduling constraints and days-available (supplied through the conversational interface, Section 8, and routed into the weekly-microcycle loop, Section 6; `research/00` AUT-04), and the current chart state (CTL/ATL, Section 3 §3.5) as the starting load from which the ramp is planned.
2. **Size the macrocycle.** Allocate base/build/peak/taper block lengths from the default split (base ≈ 50% / build ≈ 30% / peak ≈ 20% of the non-taper weeks, terminal taper ~2 weeks), adjusted by weeks-to-race, goal distance, and training age and compressed base-first on a short calendar (§5.5.1, §5.5.4), placing the taper as the terminal block (Section 7 owns its depth) and the recovery-week slots in the mesocycle rhythm (§5.5.2).
3. **Assign each mesocycle a target determinant** by the addressability scoring (§5.7), converging the peak on race-specific work regardless (§5.7.3).
4. **Set the intensity distribution per phase** — ~80% Zone 1 throughout, pyramidal→polarizing Z2/Z3 split by phase and distance (§5.6).
5. **Build each microcycle** from a template honoring the phase intensity distribution and the recovery-time-course spacing, anchoring the long run and spacing hard sessions by `recovery_cost_tag` (§5.5.3), with weekly volume progressed under a ramp-rate that Section 6's guardrails bound (the ramp is planned here; the cap is enforced there — Section 6 §6.2.2 ships the concrete weekly-CTL-rise band).
6. **Instantiate and anchor each workout** from the typology (§5.3), storing every intensity relative to the state anchors (§5.4) with target bands (§5.2.5) and secondary HR/RPE anchors where decoupling is expected (§5.4.4).
7. **Emit the plan** as the ordered set of §5.2 workout objects with their metadata, confidences, and provenance (which determinant each block targets and why), so Section 6 can adapt it and Section 9 can explain it.

**The outbound push.** Executable workouts are published to the athlete's device through the Garmin **Training API** channel Section 2 identified (§2.7.1): the generator maps each workout's segment list into the API's structured-workout format — its step/repeat structure, per-step target types (pace/HR/power range or open/lap-button), and per-step durations — resolving each relative `intensity` to an absolute `resolved_target` at push time from the current anchor value (§5.2.2), and publishes the workouts and the plan (and, where relevant, the goal course) to the athlete's Connect calendar, from which they sync to the watch and execute step-by-step. The push is the outbound counterpart to Section 2's post-session ingestion, closing the plan half of the loop; the representation is Section 5's, the channel and its authentication are Section 2's. Because the push resolves relative targets to absolutes only at push time, a workout re-pushed after a state-model update carries the updated paces automatically, with no plan edit — the relative-anchoring payoff (§5.4.3) extends all the way to the device.

## 5.9 What this section hands to the rest of the system

Section 5 delivers the plan generator and the objects it produces: a single machine-representable workout structure (§5.2) — ordered segments with relatively-anchored intensity specs, duration/quantity specs, nested set structure, and metadata carrying the target determinant, phase, target bands, and recovery-cost tag — spanning the full workout typology (§5.3); a relative-anchored pace prescription and three-zone system built from Section 4's threshold/CS/vVO₂max anchors that re-anchors itself automatically when the state model updates (§5.4); a periodized base→build→peak→taper structure parameterized by distance, training age, and goal-race conditions (§5.5); the pyramidal→polarizing, ~80%-easy intensity distribution with a tunable Z2/Z3 split (§5.6); and the determinant-targeted generation logic that concentrates each block on the largest addressable gap-to-goal, weighted by trainability, time-to-race, and confidence (§5.7). It also fixes the two typology parameters upstream sections read — the qualifying-long-run definition Section 3's durability metric consumes (§3.8) and the qualifying-maximal-effort definition Section 4's state model consumes (§4.4.1, §4.7) — closing those forward references (§5.2.6).

Four explicit hand-offs carry forward. **Section 6** reads the generated plan and adapts it — gating hard days by readiness, re-anchoring the next sessions to fresh state estimates, bounding the planned ramp with its guardrails, firing the injury-risk override, and calling this generator to re-periodize when a material state or goal change warrants it — under the arbitration ladder (`research/00` ARB-01). **Section 7** takes the taper block Section 5 placed and owns its depth, exponential shape, and individualization by chronic load and distance, plus the mandatory recovery-week guardrails Section 5 slotted. **Section 3** scores the completion-vs-prescription of every executed workout against the target bands Section 5 set (§3.2), and reads the qualifying-long-run gate (§3.8) Section 5 defines. **Section 4** reads the qualifying-maximal-effort tags Section 5 assigns to identify which sessions become critical-speed, vVO₂max, threshold, and durability observations (§4.4.1), and the generator in turn deliberately schedules time trials and tune-up races to supply Section 4 the fresh maximal efforts it needs when a determinant's confidence has decayed (§5.2.6). The division of labor with the neighboring sections is preserved throughout: Section 4 estimates the athlete, Section 1 turns that estimate into the pace target and the gap to goal, Section 5 turns the gap and the athlete into a periodized, determinant-targeted, relatively-anchored plan, and Section 6 turns that plan into what the athlete does each day.
